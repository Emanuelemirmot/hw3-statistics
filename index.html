09 / Interpretation and theoretical background

Model. Let X₁, …, Xₙ be i.i.d. increments for one attacker. The total penetration score is Sₙ = ΣXᵢ, the average is Sₙ/n, and the normalized total is Sₙ/√n. We simulate m independent copies of this process, representing m attackers.

PropertyBernoulli(p)Rademacher
μ = E[X]p0
σ² = Var(X)p(1−p)1
E[Sₙ]np0
Var(Sₙ)np(1−p)n
Var(Sₙ/n)p(1−p)/n1/n
Var(Sₙ/√n)p(1−p)1

Law of Large Numbers. For both models, Sₙ/n converges in probability to μ. In the plots, the running averages settle near p for Bernoulli and 0 for Rademacher. The variance of the average shrinks as 1/n.

Central Limit Theorem. For Rademacher, μ=0 and σ²=1, so Sₙ/√n converges in distribution to N(0,1). For Bernoulli with 0<p<1, the correct standardization is (Sₙ−np)/√(np(1−p)), which converges to N(0,1). The uncentered Bernoulli Sₙ/√n has mean p√n and does not converge to a fixed standard normal distribution when p>0.

Cybersecurity interpretation. Each step represents interaction with another system; a Bernoulli 1 can mean successful penetration and 0 unsuccessful penetration. Rademacher +1/−1 represents a symmetric gain/loss score, not a literal count of compromised systems. Increasing n changes the score's scale: totals spread as √n around their means while averages become more stable. Increasing m provides more independent trajectories and smoother empirical distributions, but does not change the per-attacker theoretical distribution.

This is a stylized mathematical model: i.i.d. increments assume no adaptation, shared vulnerabilities, attacker coordination, or dependence between systems. Real penetration attempts can violate these assumptions.

Suggested experiments. Compare n=20, 100, 500, 2000 at m=500; repeat with m=50 and m=2000; switch Bernoulli p between 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8. Observe variance shrinkage and the CLT reference fit. The simulation is educational, not a predictive security risk model.
